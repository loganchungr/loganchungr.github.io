# on Labatut’s MANIAC

**maybe the only man I'd trust to write an ostensibly literary novel about the iPhone.**

before i get to rambling, some more direct thoughts on *The MANIAC:*

- the writing is solid throughout, but I found its central portion lacking and a bit meandersome.
- i thought the various voices could have been more distinct.
- the epistolary format was interesting, maybe he was trying to mimic something of a documentary feel, but ultimately I'm not sure that it really served to benefit Labatut's central thesis —
- — which, stated simply, is that John von Neumann Matters A Lot And Was Ahead Of His Time. and for the most part i was like, okay. not sure this is how i wanted to be persuaded of that.
- kind of a Sebaldy-Bolaño-Lovecraft thing for people who believe that we've sipped from some truly ancient evil goblets with all this science we've been doing the last century or two.
- I am excited for his next effort, as I think he has "the stuff" to make something Pretty Important if he can find the right distance to focus his lens, and maybe a way to evolve his approach beyond the dramatized Wikipedia/docu-series vibe here.

at it's best, *The MANIAC* is arresting and transportive in the way that I remember him achieving with *When We Cease to Understand the World* . the third and final section about AlphaGo is the best part, but one wonders how many, if any, of the preceding pages were really necessary for it to succeed. i suppose that if you simply took Labatut's adulation of Von Neumann for granted, you might get more mileage out of the book.

all that being said, I'm a Benjamin Labatut Believer, ever since I read his prior book (which i will soon revisit, given my middling feelings about this effort). i think both the questions and the methods of his overall project are worthy, important, interesting, and at their best, exciting ways to explore what literature can do in this century.

Labatut understands that artistic knowledge and scientific knowledge have more in common than they do in opposition. he also understands that what we sometimes call "divine inspiration" can feel like demonic possession — and crucially, he is able to levy his chosen medium against these false dichotomies, using prose to conjure the spiritual mysteries at the heart of both endeavors.

---

in interviews, Labatut speaks emphatically, desperately, with a pleading and almost preacherly cadence, about the essentiality of uncertainty, the sacredness of literature, and the murky, beautiful truths that can be found at the limits of human understanding.

he is skeptical of technology's ability to deliver meaning to human existence, but he is also obsessed with the technological minds who bring about revolutionary scientific and technological paradigm shifts which inform our conception of what the hell is going on in the universe.

I think Labatut would agree with my belief that our current discourse around technology is rather impoverished; to place his project along a simple axis of *techno-optimism* vs. *-pessimism* would be needlessly limiting. nor is he agnostic about these questions like a journalist might be.

instead, what Labatut's work offers is a kind of *shamanistic* approach to questions about progress. he sees a clear parallel between the pursuit of "pure" forms of knowledge — scientific, technological, digital — and the kind of creative inspiration that informs literature and poetry.

---

Labatut himself preaches that literature is a kind of demented, "fucked-up grandmother of science," and that fiction is one of the most powerful "tools" that we have to pursue truth, one which allows us to get away from the digital, discrete "facts" of the universe. one cannot digitalize every aspect of "continuous" human life.

this last quote comes from a recent appearance Labatut made at an AI conference hosted by Sana, a Swedish AI company focused on "building the next generation of knowledge tools." Sana allotted Labatut just over 15 minutes to speak onstage with Jasmine Sun, a tech reporter for The Atlantic.

it is hard for me to imagine any other modern novelist being alloted space to speak at such a conference, and it is probably good that a piece of contemporary literature is on the radar of the kinds of people who would organize or attend such an event, but it also points to Labatut's rather precarious position.

it is no leap for me to assume that the men and women in charge of our current desperate charge towards the disastrous event-horizon of AGI think of themselves as great artists — two categories of person that literature has traditionally treated with the fawning awe with which Labatut treats his scientists and mathematicians.

Labatut seems to understand that their drive is experienced by them as a kind of creative inspiration, an effort to expand the human spirit — misguided, perverse as it may be, and whether those of us who adhere to the older sacred methods choose to believe it or not.

setting aside the very valid critiques of "Great Man theories" of history for now, Labatut's ability to write about these Great Men is predicated on his simultaneous preoccupation with the fundamental folly of such efforts. "the pursuit of truth will always lead to more uncertainty," he says in an interview, after referencing Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle.

literature, fiction, and the arts fill in the gaps between the metricized, digitalized conception of human existence pursued by our greatest scientists, mathematicians, and technologists. the unexplainable highlights the fundamental hollowness of a purely rational view of nature, inside and out. one can easily foresee, however, a darkly humorous situation where the technocratic class miss this point entirely.

---

elsewhere, Labatut has spoken of the past as a vast, unexplored territory, endlessly mineable by literature in search of enrichment and meaning. writing at a temporal (and factual) distance from the men who set the course for our modern machinated malaise allows him to insert spiritual, almost demonic elements into otherwise dryly historical tales.

his inventors and researchers are mad, possessed, obsessive, irrational, more like figures from religious folklore than disciplined men of reason and dry calculatory modernity. wisdom is knowing that regardless of one's chosen method of pursuing truth — be it through science, religion, or art — what lies at the end is the same uncertainty, and that itself is truth, and that truth is the one essential fact of our fragile existence Down Here.

placed in front of the contemporary counterparts, however, one can feel Labatut's discomfort. watching him onstage at Sana, Labatut's usual self-assuredness is replaced with shakiness. he fidgets constantly with his pant leg. "What do you mean when you say human beings are *continuous*?" Sun asks him. later, Labatut tells his audience that they should be thinking more about the gods.

one wonders what the tech workers in the audience might have made of this. these are the Millennials and Gen Z laptop drones who have inherited the reins of progress from his characters. here, Labatut's impassioned tone sounds more like exasperation. it is the fundamental friction of his project with another brittle fact of life on earth: sometimes, the most powerful people are not spiritual at all, nor are they passionate, principled, or even inspired — sometimes, they are simply *stupid*.
