# on Natalia Ginzburg’s Voices in the Evening (1961)

solid 4. a lovely return to the stolid plaintive melancholy of Ginzburg's prose, complete with the monologue-as-dialogue device she'd proceed to perfect in The Family Lexicon. these two feel very much in conversation with each other, and her author's note (which Alexander Chee also enjoys) about regretting that these characters are fictional, having loved them so, makes it clear why she so quickly pivoted to her own personal life.

they share much in common: vignettes of family backstory and a tapestry of the tragedies, familial idioms, and political turbulence (loved Tóibín in the intro stating it as "politics as manners, not just morals") that defined life for most provincial Italians at the time. what's somewhat ironic is that whereas that while The Family Lexicon omits Ginzburg almost entirely as a character herself, Elsa, the narrator of Voices in the Evening, looms large in the story —— primarily through her near total omission for the first half of the book, which focuses entirely on the factory-owning De Francisci family.

then, as the second half opens, a kind of "reveal" occurs, when Ginzburg mentions in her classically beautiful blasé style the romance between Elsa and Tommasino. the depth and intimacy of the details divulged by Elsa of Tommasino's family life and history, including that of all his siblings and in-laws and parents, serves as a fucking genius foundation upon which we too are carrying in our hearts as the gulf between their experiences of the affair become clear. so, too, are the ways that the political/historical backdrop mirrors that of so many of these characters and their village: "Why has everything been ruined?"

I'm not sure how she wins me over, but somewhere in the music of this writing Ginzburg is able to sell me on the melancholy, the little tragedies and humors of everyday life, and here, even some romantic melodrama. the world feels lived in and the characters feel real (much more so than, say, Ferrante's novels) — maybe part of the reason I will return more readily to Family Lexicon, though I can see literally no harm in just assuming these characters are just as real, too. fiction really can make untruths as real as truth.

lastly: one has to wonder, in her descriptions of ambivalent relationships and marriages, what she is leaning back on, so many years after the tragic loss of her own husband, about whom she seemed to have had a long-running push-pull (see: "the right person" passage from "Human Relationships" in "The Little Virtues") of surety. how does one step forward from that and address one's feelings after such a fiery, tragic ending? also: RIP Vincenzino, a brilliant and tragic passage.

I love this writing and I love this writer. I can't wait to read more. Reading Natalia Ginzburg does what my absolute favorite fiction and essays do: it makes me a better, more patient, more gracious, more observant participant in history, in friendship, and in my communities. Her writing, in its plainness and rhythm and reality, inspires me to find the music in all of these things, the beautiful and the ugly.
