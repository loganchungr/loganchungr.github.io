# on DeLillo & revisiting Lockwood

## on *White Noise*

*White Noise* was a slightly different book than I was expecting but I very much enjoyed reading it. Reports of an Airborne Toxic Event-driven plot are greatly exaggerated. In the book, DeLillo paints a portrait of a (vaguely) contemporary society roughly thus:

A world where corporations have supplanted God, where the ancient fear of death is repressed via rampant consumerism rather than transcended via faith; where language has been so stripped of meaning, so diluted in a society saturated with the noise of labels and talk show babble and theories of everything, that language itself has become a mere simulacra of itself rather than communicating essential things about reality, or reflecting it from you to me — and knowing this, knowing the causes and the effects and yet helpless to stop any of it, we sit back in rapt awe and gaze at the most brilliant, terrific, spectacular sunsets mankind has ever seen, made possible by our glorious perversion of Nature throughout the American century, and holding within them death at a grander scale than any one of us could dare to imagine.

DeLillo does this through the most glancingly incisive prose, forays into wooden philosophical dialogue exchanges (even with children), and scenes of brief human familial sweetness, all punctuated by occasional laugh-out-loud moments of dry humor. So if all that sounds up your alley, crack it open and enjoy!

As someone who struggles with the zaniness and *arcanity* of Pynchon — perhaps because I am stupid, or too overstimulated by modern society, or had the disprivilege of being born too steeped in that language-sodden world DeLillo inferred in this book — I am glad to have embarked upon my first DeLillan dose of (post)modern American skepticism, even if I have a creeping unease about his philosophy that will need to be further excavated.

## bridge-beat…

Perhaps one of the most interesting elements of *White Noise* (this can probably apply to *IJ* as well) is how well it captures the humdrum feeling of overstimulation that defines contemporary existence, despite rarely touching directly on the sources of this overstimulation and despite predating the Internet by a decade. DeLillo will occasionally mention the radio and television, but even the nature of the longer conversations between characters begins to feel familiar — it’s almost like they are supplying noise for one another; they are waiting for podcasts to be invented and so they must make their own philosophical babble in the meantime.

Lockwood, meanwhile, fully suffused in a drastically more saturated informational environment, struggles to paint as convincing of a picture. Is she simply too close? Is it possible through fiction or prose to capture the feeling of swimming in this stuff, this “white noise”? Imagine taking your fingers to a screen full of static — the visual equivalent of white noise — and combing through it as you might part a doorway covered by streamers and tinsel. But there’s nothing inside, just more noise, more sparkle, more stuff to comb through. Better, perhaps, or at least easier, to back away and focus on what’s happening outside the frame.

## revisiting Patricia Lockwood’s *No One is Talking About This*

…jotting down this review during a week in which everyone in "The Portal" — as our narrator might say — is reflecting on "Woke 1," and how it was "crazy." I first read this book shortly after its release, when Woke 1 was in its denouement, and when much of the Portal's discourse around the book centered on how Patricia Lockwood had purportedly managed to capture in prose The Experience of Being Online These Days.

with that metric as a judge, I left my first reading being quite disappointed, and this reread was no different — I speak, however, of disappointment only in the first half of the book. the second half saves this story, if not the Project, and it does so gloriously. anyways the first half of the narrative consists of our narrator being Too Online; her entire experience is constructed, and deranged by, posts and memes and discourse and etc etc.... but I for one never got the vibe that Lockwood captured or understood the actual mindset or experience of this kind of person very well.

the portrait painted of our narrator in Part I feels like a confused amalgamation of various archetypes of the Serially Online Girl, none of which add up to a coherent understanding of any particular type of actual existing person. arbitrary metaphors dangle at the start of sentences, unfulfilled by their ends, or elsewhere they just-as-arbitrarily slap you in the face at the conclusion of a paragraph.

the most successful moments in Part I find Lockwood zooming out and speaking almost anthropologically; the subject of the paragraph will sometimes become the plural first-person We, like a demented continuance of Annie Ernaux's The Years (The years). in these moments, our narrator is assumedly so subsumed by the hivemind of The Portal that Lockwood, behind the IRL keys, emerges like some kind of alien-from-real-life into the narrative — and what better way to paint a portrait of how it felt to experience "Woke I" online than this entirely subjugated, sublimated, subjectified-to-the-point-of-erasure narrative stance?

but unfortunately, for the most part, Part I feel like a cheap setup for Part II, which was even more beautiful than I remember, and if the entire point of this book is an exercise in contrasts, then I suppose she's succeeded, because the narrator's shift is so stark, our protagonist now so whole and fully realized, that it makes me wonder whether all the slop and muddiness of Part I wasn't purposeful after all.

I wonder also whether the rapid-fire, very contemporary form will age well either; it felt as arbitrary as the book's bifurcated dialogic structure.... these two things did not need to go together, and if they had to, I wish the first half had been anywhere near as organized, beautiful, or at least thoughtful as the conclusion.
